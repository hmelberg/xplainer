::: new_page(title="Flip the mirror", transition=wipe-left)

::: write_speak
Lectures 3 to 5 were about the demand side — patients and insurers. Now flip the mirror: the person who writes the prescription has their own incentives too.

::: new_page(title="Two principals, one agent", transition=fade)

::: message(level=info, icon=stethoscope)
The physician is supposed to act in the patient's interest. But she also has an income, a schedule, a mortgage, and a malpractice lawyer. **Two principals (patient + payer), one agent (doctor).** That gap is the whole supply-side story.

::: new_page(title="Fuchs 1978: the tonsil puzzle", transition=cross-dissolve)

::: write
**Victor Fuchs (1978):** across US regions, per-capita tonsillectomy rates tracked the number of surgeons per capita — not the number of bad tonsils. More surgeons, more surgeries.

::: table
| Region | Surgeons per 100k | Tonsillectomies per 1,000 kids |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 4.1 | 2.1 |
| B | 5.2 | 2.6 |
| C | 5.8 | 2.9 |
| D | 6.7 | 3.4 |
| E | 7.5 | 3.9 |
| F | 8.3 | 4.5 |
| G | 9.1 | 4.8 |
| H | 10.0 | 5.3 |
| I | 11.2 | 6.0 |
| J | 12.4 | 6.7 |

::: write_speak
Double the surgeons, roughly double the tonsils coming out. Supply seems to create its own demand — economists call this **supplier-induced demand**.

::: new_page(title="Three ways to pay a doctor", transition=slide-up)

::: table
| Unit of payment | What it rewards | Classic risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fee-for-service** — per procedure | Volume: more visits, more scans, more surgeries | Over-provision, supplier-induced demand |
| **Capitation** — per enrolled patient per month | Keeping the patient healthy and out of the clinic | Under-provision, cherry-picking healthy enrollees |
| **DRG / case-mix** — per discharge by diagnosis | Short inpatient stays, efficient throughput | "Quicker and sicker" discharges |

::: write_speak
Each payment unit is a different steering wheel — none of them is neutral. You pick your incentive, you pick your distortion.

::: new_page(title="Kaiser vs Medicare", transition=fade)

::: message(level=info, icon=building-hospital)
**Kaiser Permanente** uses salaried physicians plus capitation inside an integrated delivery system. For comparable populations, it runs **roughly 20–30% lower procedure rates than fee-for-service Medicare** — same medicine, different steering wheel.

::: new_page(title="Where does Sovaldi fit?", transition=cross-dissolve)

::: message(level=warn, icon=pill)
Specialists can't really induce demand for **Sovaldi** — a Hep C diagnosis is objective, and you either have the virus or you don't. But hospitals CAN negotiate rebates, and pharmacists CAN steer patients to preferred brands. **Incentive design matters at every link in the chain.**

::: new_page(title="Check yourself", transition=wipe-right)

::: multiple_choice
A hospital paid by DRG (per discharge by diagnosis group) has a strong incentive to...
* discharge patients quickly, sometimes before they are fully recovered
- admit as many patients as possible regardless of diagnosis
- prevent enrolled patients from getting sick in the first place

::: celebrate(kind=confetti, bursts=2)
::: sound(name=success)

::: write_speak
Right — DRGs pay a fixed lump per discharge, so every extra hospital day is pure cost. "Admit more" is the fee-for-service reflex; "prevent illness" is the capitation reflex.

::: new_page(title="Zooming back out", transition=slide-up)

::: write_speak
Demand-side tools change what patients face; supply-side payment rules change what doctors face. A smart health system tunes both dials at once.
