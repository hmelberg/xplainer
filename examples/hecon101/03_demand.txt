## The RAND Health Insurance Experiment
- 1974–1982, funded by the US government
- **2,750 families**, randomly assigned
- Goal: does cost-sharing change how much care people use?

::: mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[2,750 families] --> B[Free care]
    A --> C[25% coshare]
    A --> D[50% coshare]
    A --> E[95% coshare]
    A --> F[HMO arm]
    B --> G[How much care used?]
    C --> G
    D --> G
    E --> G
    F --> G

::: write_speak
Families were randomized to five insurance arms, from fully free to nearly full cost-sharing. Then RAND watched them get sick for up to five years.

::: new_page(title="What RAND found", transition=cross-dissolve)

::: table
| Plan        | Patient pays | Relative spending |
| ----------- | ------------ | ----------------- |
| Free        | 0%           | 1.00              |
| 25% coshare | 25%          | 0.87              |
| 50% coshare | 50%          | 0.82              |
| 95% coshare | 95%          | 0.69              |

::: write
When people paid nothing, they used the most care. When they paid almost everything, they used about 30% less. No surprise — but *how much* less is the interesting part.

::: new_page(title="Putting a number on it", transition=fade)

::: math
\varepsilon = \frac{\%\,\Delta Q}{\%\,\Delta P} \approx -0.2

::: write
**Translation:** a 10% price increase reduces demand by about 2%. That is *inelastic* — the same ballpark as cigarettes, gasoline, and electricity.

::: new_page(title="Why so unresponsive?", transition=wipe-right)

::: write
## Why health care demand is inelastic
- **Urgency** — you don't price-shop during a heart attack.
- **Expert recommendation** — the doctor says "you need this", and you mostly say yes.
- **Insurance buffering** — the insurer pays most of the bill anyway.
- **No substitutes** — there is no generic version of "not dying".

::: message(level=info, icon=pill)
**Sovaldi flashback.** Sovaldi has no cheaper substitute and no price competition. Its demand is close to *perfectly inelastic* — which is exactly why pricing at €60,000 was rational for Gilead.

::: new_page(title="A modern confirmation", transition=slide-up)

::: write_speak
In 2008 Oregon ran a Medicaid lottery and randomly gave coverage to some uninsured adults. Winners used about 30% more care than losers — mostly ER and outpatient visits — confirming RAND's direction a generation later.

::: new_page(title="Check your intuition", transition=iris)

::: write
An elasticity of **-0.2** means a 20% price increase will reduce demand by approximately…

::: multiple_choice
Pick the closest answer.
- 20%
* 4%
- 200%
- nothing at all

::: celebrate(kind=confetti, bursts=2)
::: sound(name=success)

::: write_speak
Four percent. Multiply the 20% price change by the elasticity of 0.2, and you get a demand drop of about one-fifth the price change.

::: new_page(title="Wrap-up", transition=fade)

::: write
## What to remember
- RAND HIE: elasticity of demand for health care ≈ **-0.2**
- Demand is *inelastic* — price nudges it, but does not shove it
- Oregon HIE (2008) confirmed the same direction with a natural experiment
- Which means: price signals alone cannot control health spending

::: write_speak
If patients barely flinch at price, the interesting action happens elsewhere — in how insurance is designed. That is exactly where we go next.
