Lecture 7 gave us the ethics; this lecture gives us the arithmetic. One number decides more drug approvals in Europe than any other: the ICER.

::: new_page(title="What's a QALY?", transition=fade)

::: write(effect=fade)
**QALY = Quality-Adjusted Life Year**

A year of life, weighted by health-related quality.

Scale: **0 = dead**, **1 = perfect health**.

A year spent at quality 0.5 → **0.5 QALYs**.

::: draw(size=420, animate=true)

::: new_page(title="The ICER formula", transition=cross-dissolve)

::: write_speak
The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio asks one thing: how much extra cash buys one extra QALY?

::: math
\text{ICER} = \frac{C_B - C_A}{Q_B - Q_A}

::: message(level=info, icon=lightbulb)
Read it as "euros per QALY gained". Lower = better value.

::: new_page(title="Sovaldi by the numbers", transition=wipe-right)

::: table
| Option        | Cost     | QALYs |
| ------------- | -------- | ----- |
| Old treatment | €10,000  | 4     |
| Sovaldi       | €60,000  | 9     |
| **Δ (extra)** | **€50,000** | **5** |
| **ICER**      | **€10,000 / QALY** |   |

::: write_speak
Fifty thousand extra euros buys five extra QALYs, so Sovaldi costs about ten thousand euros per QALY gained. Now we compare that number to a threshold.

::: new_page(title="Compare to the threshold", transition=fade)

::: write_speak
At €10,000 per QALY, Sovaldi sits well below the €30,000 per QALY reference threshold. By pure CEA logic, it's a no-brainer.

Sovaldi lands in the northeast quadrant — more expensive, but enough extra QALYs to stay under the threshold line. That's the quadrant where CEA actually has a job to do.

::: message(level=warn, icon=alert)
CEA tells you "per-patient" efficiency. It does NOT tell you "can we afford it for 200,000 patients" — that's Budget Impact Analysis, coming in Lecture 9.
