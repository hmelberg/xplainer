::: write_speak
In 2014, Gilead launched Sovaldi — an oral pill that cures hepatitis C in more than 95% of patients. The price per 12-week course: sixty thousand euros.

::: message(level=info, icon=pill)
**Sovaldi (sofosbuvir), 2014** — cure rate >95%, list price ≈ €60,000 per course. With 3-5 million Hep C patients in Europe, a full rollout would have eaten national drug budgets whole.

::: write_speak
Standard microeconomics has a tidy answer to high prices: substitute, postpone, or shop around. Watch all three answers fail.

::: write(effect=fade, split=true, split_mode=sentence)
**Substitute?** There is no cheaper cure. The old interferon regimen was toxic and often failed.
**Postpone?** Hepatitis C quietly scars your liver while you wait. "Next year" is not neutral.
**Shop around?** Only your hepatologist can prescribe it, and the price is set by one manufacturer.

::: new_page(title="So what breaks?", transition=wipe-left)

::: write_speak
Four features of health care bend the standard supply-and-demand picture out of shape. Every lecture in this course is really about one of them.

**One-line examples — the four gaps:**
- **Uncertainty:** You don't schedule your heart attack.
- **Asymmetric info:** Your cardiologist knows if the stent is optional.
- **Externalities:** Your flu shot protects me.
- **Equity:** A society that tolerates "priced out of chemo" is not one we live in.

::: new_page(title="Back to Sovaldi, briefly", transition=cross-dissolve)

::: write_speak
All four gaps show up in one drug. You can't predict who gets Hep C, the specialist knows more than the patient, untreated carriers infect others, and "only rich patients get cured" is politically radioactive.

::: message(level=warn, icon=compass)
If the first four lectures feel like a tour of market failures, that is the point. Health economics is the study of a market that admits, up front, that it is not a normal market.
