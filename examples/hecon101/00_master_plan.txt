# Master plan — hecon101: Introduction to Health Economics

A 10-lecture xplainer series introducing master's students to health economics.
Each lecture is short (5-7 minutes of playtime), visually rich, and grounded in
**named real-world examples**. Narration is kept light — prefer silent `::: write`
for definitions and formulas, reserve `::: write_speak` for the 1-2 key sentences
that really need a voice.

## Audience

Master's students in public health, economics, health policy, health management,
and medicine. Assumes introductory microeconomics and basic statistics. No prior
health economics required.

## The running case: the Sovaldi debate

Every lecture circles back to one concrete case: **Sovaldi (sofosbuvir)**, an oral
drug launched by Gilead in 2014 that **cures hepatitis C in >95% of patients**.
A 12-week course was priced at **€60,000-84,000 per patient**. With 3-5 million
Hep C patients in Europe, a full rollout would have consumed national drug
budgets. Every health system had to decide: cover it for whom, on what terms, at
what price?

This one case illustrates nearly every idea in the course — demand, insurance,
market failure, cost-effectiveness, equity, budget impact — so it threads all
ten lectures together. Use it as the anchor example at least once per lecture.

## The ten lectures

### 01 — Why health economics is different
**File**: `01_why_different.txt`. Hook: Sovaldi priced a cure at €60,000. If this
were a car or a phone, "demand = supply curve intersection" would close the case.
But here, standard microeconomics feels broken. Why? Four distinguishing features:
**uncertainty** (you don't know when you'll need care), **asymmetric information**
(the doctor knows more than you), **externalities** (vaccinating you protects me),
**equity concerns** (nobody accepts "priced out of chemo"). Set up the course as
a guided tour of how economists handle these four gaps.

### 02 — Health as investment (Grossman model)
**File**: `02_grossman.txt`. Health is a stock that depreciates over time. You
inherit some, you produce more via time and medical care, it decays (fast when
old, slow when young). Grossman (1972): demand for health is demand for an
investment good AND a consumption good. Demand for health CARE is *derived* from
demand for health. One simple diagram: stock over age, with investment peaks
keeping it above a critical threshold. Keep the math to one line.

### 03 — Demand for health care
**File**: `03_demand.txt`. Price elasticity of demand. The **RAND Health
Insurance Experiment** (1974-1982, 2,750 families, randomized to cost-sharing
arms): elasticity ≈ **-0.2**. The **Oregon Health Insurance Experiment** (2008
Medicaid lottery): effect of coverage on ER visits and outpatient use. Why
health-care demand is inelastic: you don't price-shop during a heart attack.
Moral hazard exists but it's moderate in magnitude.

### 04 — Insurance and moral hazard
**File**: `04_insurance.txt`. Why people buy insurance: risk aversion +
diminishing marginal utility of income. Ex-ante moral hazard (less prevention
because insurance pays if you get sick) vs ex-post moral hazard (more care
because it's free at the point of use). Cost-sharing tools: deductibles,
coinsurance, copays. Sovaldi example: if you have a €5,000 deductible, does that
matter when the drug costs €60,000? Oregon HIE result: Medicaid raised ER use
despite cost-sharing — moral hazard exists.

### 05 — Adverse selection and market failure
**File**: `05_adverse_selection.txt`. Akerlof's lemons model, applied to
insurance: if insurer can't see risk, premium → average cost → healthy drop out
→ pool gets sicker → premium rises → death spiral. Fixes: individual mandate
(ACA, Swiss Santésuisse, German system), risk adjustment, community rating,
reinsurance. Example: 2017 debate on ACA mandate repeal.

### 06 — Supply side: physician and hospital incentives
**File**: `06_supply.txt`. Physician as imperfect agent for the patient.
Supplier-induced demand (Fuchs 1978 tonsillectomy study; per-capita surgeries
track surgeon density). Payment systems compared: **fee-for-service** (pays for
volume), **capitation** (pays for enrolled patients), **DRG / case-mix** (pays
per discharge). Example: Kaiser Permanente (capitation + integration) vs
traditional Medicare (fee-for-service). What each system incentivizes.

### 07 — Equity vs efficiency
**File**: `07_equity.txt`. Two ethical lenses: **Rawlsian** (maximize the
minimum) vs **utilitarian** (maximize the total). Horizontal equity = equal
treatment for equal need; vertical equity = appropriate differentiation by need.
Orphan drugs create the classic tension: €500,000 per QALY for a rare disease —
CEA says no, equity argument says yes. Sovaldi tie-in: should Hep C treatment
priority go to the sickest, the youngest, or those with children at home?

### 08 — Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA)
**File**: `08_cea.txt`. The formula: **ICER = (Cost_B - Cost_A) / (QALY_B -
QALY_A)**. What a QALY is: a year of life weighted by health-related quality
(0 = dead, 1 = perfect health). Compute Sovaldi's ICER from the shared numbers
(€60,000 cost, 5 QALYs gained → €12,000/QALY) and compare to the €30,000/QALY
threshold. The cost-effectiveness plane (quadrants) as a picture.

### 09 — Decision-making: thresholds, BIA, MCDA
**File**: `09_decisions.txt`. Who actually decides what gets covered? **NICE**
(UK, £20-30k/QALY threshold), **IQWiG** (Germany, no explicit threshold),
**HAS** (France, therapeutic benefit scale), **ICER** (US, voluntary). Key move:
even if CEA says yes, **Budget Impact Analysis** can say no — Sovaldi's ICER is
great per patient, but rolling it out to 200,000 patients costs €12 billion.
MCDA for factors CEA can't price (severity, equity, innovation).

### 10 — Case study: the Sovaldi decision
**File**: `10_sovaldi_case.txt`. The finale. Walk through how three different
countries handled Sovaldi: **UK** (NHS price negotiation, phased rollout),
**France** (conditional reimbursement restricted to sickest first), **US** (state
Medicaid rationing by fibrosis stage, lawsuits). Critique each using the tools
from Lectures 1-9. Discuss what a "just" answer would look like. Loop back to
the four distinguishing features from Lecture 1.
