::: new_page(title="A strange question", transition=fade)

::: write_speak
In 1972 an economist named Michael Grossman asked a strange question: what if health is a *capital good*, like a car or a house? The answer turns out to shape almost every modern health policy debate.

::: new_page(title="Recap from Lecture 1", transition=cross-dissolve)

::: message(level=info, icon=link)
Last lecture we saw four reasons health markets misbehave: uncertainty, asymmetric information, externalities, and equity. Grossman gives us the first coherent theory that takes at least two of them seriously.

::: new_page(title="The four claims", transition=wipe-left)

::: write
**Grossman's model in four claims**

1. You inherit a stock of health at birth.
2. The stock depreciates — faster as you age.
3. You invest in it with time and medical care.
4. There is an optimal path over the lifecycle.

::: new_page(title="The one-line law of motion", transition=wipe-right)

::: math
H_t = (1 - \delta)\, H_{t-1} + I_t

::: write
Health tomorrow = health today, minus what depreciates, plus what you invest. One line. That is the whole engine.

::: new_page(title="The derived-demand twist", transition=iris)

::: message(level=info, icon=lightbulb)
You do not want an MRI. You do not want a pill. You want the *health* those things produce. Demand for health CARE is **derived** from demand for health itself.

::: new_page(title="Two 40-year-olds", transition=cross-dissolve)

::: table
| Person              | Age | δ (yearly decay) | Lifestyle investment |
| ------------------- | --- | ---------------- | -------------------- |
| Runner, non-smoker  | 40  | ~1.5%            | High (time + diet)   |
| Heavy smoker        | 40  | ~4.0%            | Low                  |

::: write
Same calendar age. Very different biological trajectories. In Grossman's language: the smoker's δ is bigger, so the same investment buys less health.

::: new_page(title="Sovaldi, rethought", transition=fade)

::: write_speak
For a Hep C patient, Sovaldi isn't consumption. It's capital investment — restoring a health stock that was quietly depreciating for years.

::: new_page(title="Check your intuition", transition=slide-up)

::: multiple_choice
Why does Grossman's model predict older people consume MORE health care than younger people, even though they have less life ahead?
* Because depreciation δ is higher in old age, so more investment is needed to keep the stock above the floor.
- Because insurance premiums are cheaper for older people, so care is a better deal.
- Because older people have more free time and spend it at the doctor.
