::: write_speak
Every rate ratio, every hazard ratio, every Kaplan-Meier curve you will ever see is built on a single decision per person: when does their clock start, and when does it stop?

::: write_speak
Get those two dates right and everything else falls into place. Get them wrong and your answer silently lies to you. This whole lecture is about that quiet little backbone.

::: new_page(title="Three ways the clock stops", transition=fade)

::: write
**Follow-up ends at the earliest of three events:**
- The outcome of interest happens (success — or disaster, depending).
- The person dies of something else (censored).
- The study database runs out of time — ours ends **2023-12-31** (censored).

::: speak
Three things can end follow-up. The event of interest, death from some other cause, or the study simply ending. The earliest of the three wins. Remember that word — earliest.

::: write_speak
Remember from Lecture 4, the index date is the first-ever E11 diagnosis for each person in the cohort. That is where their clock starts. Not their birth, not the start of the registry — their index date.

::: write_speak
And remember from Lecture 7, we already found the first cardiovascular hospitalisation per person. That is one of our three possible end-of-follow-up events.

::: new_page(title="Computing the end date", transition=fade)

::: r(auto_run=before, title="Computing follow-up per person")
library(dplyr)
library(lubridate)
# cohort already has person_id and index_date from Lecture 4
# first_outcome_date comes from Lecture 7 (NA if no event)
followup <- cohort |>
  left_join(person |> select(person_id, death_date), by = "person_id") |>
  mutate(
    # pmin picks the EARLIEST of its arguments, row by row
    end_date = pmin(
      first_outcome_date,          # the cardiovascular event, if any
      death_date,                  # death from any other cause, if any
      ymd("2023-12-31"),           # administrative end of the study
      na.rm = TRUE                 # ignore NAs so they do not poison the min
    ),
    # 1 if the event happened on or before end_date, 0 otherwise
    outcome_flag = as.integer(!is.na(first_outcome_date) &
                              first_outcome_date == end_date)
  )

::: write_speak
Three candidates go into `pmin`, and the smallest date wins. The `na.rm = TRUE` is essential — without it, a single missing death date would turn the whole answer into `NA`.

::: message(level=warn, icon=alert)
**Three traps that eat PhD students alive:**

1. Counting follow-up *before* the index date. That is immortal-time bias — the person could not possibly have had the event yet, so you are stacking the deck.
2. Forgetting to censor at death. People who die of unrelated causes are not "event-free" forever — they are just gone.
3. Using calendar time instead of time-since-index. Two patients with the same follow-up length should contribute the same amount, regardless of when they entered.

::: new_page(title="The Surv object", transition=slide-up)

::: write_speak
In R, time-to-event data is almost always wrapped in a `Surv` object from the `survival` package. It bundles the duration and the event indicator into one column that every survival function understands.

::: r(auto_run=before, title="Wrapping it in Surv")
library(survival)
followup <- followup |>
  mutate(
    time_years = as.numeric(end_date - index_date) / 365.25,  # days to years
    surv_obj = Surv(time = time_years, event = outcome_flag)   # the magic pair
  )
# time is in years, event is 1 if the outcome happened, 0 if censored

::: write_speak
Two numbers per person. Time in years, and a zero or a one. That is all a Kaplan-Meier curve or a Cox model needs — everything else they compute themselves.

::: new_page(title="Person-years", transition=fade)

::: r(auto_run=before, title="Total person-time")
# Add up all the follow-up durations in the cohort
sum(as.numeric(followup$end_date - followup$index_date)) / 365.25
#> [1] 7450.2

::: write_speak
Seven thousand four hundred fifty person-years of follow-up. That is like 745 people watched for 10 years, or 1490 people watched for 5 years — same information content. Person-time is the denominator of every rate you will ever compute.

::: multiple_choice
Patient A has index date 2015-06-01, died of an unrelated cause on 2018-09-15, and never had a cardiovascular event. Their follow-up is:
* from 2015-06-01 to 2018-09-15, event = 0 (censored at death)
- from 2015-06-01 to 2023-12-31, event = 0 (they made it to study end)
- zero, because they died before the study ended
- from 2015-06-01 to 2018-09-15, event = 1 (death counts as the event)
