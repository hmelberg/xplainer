# Master plan — Health + R: Data Science on Health Registries

A 10-lecture xplainer series that introduces researchers and analysts to data
science with R on **patient registry** and **prescription registry** data.
Inspired by Nordic-style national health registries where every contact with
the healthcare system is linked by a personal identifier, enabling population-
scale observational research.

## Audience

Researchers, analysts, clinicians, and students who know some R (or are ready
to pick it up) and want to work with real health data. Not for beginners who
have never seen a data frame — we assume you can install packages and read a
line of code. But each lecture is self-contained and explains what it uses.

## The running clinical example

Across the whole series we use **type 2 diabetes** and **metformin** as the
anchor. Everyone has heard of them, the clinical story is simple, and the
relevant codes are well known:

- ICD-10 for type 2 diabetes: **E11** (often **E11.9** without complications).
- ATC for metformin: **A10BA02**.
- Questions we work toward:
  - How many new cases of type 2 diabetes per year?
  - What share of patients start metformin within 90 days of diagnosis?
  - Is metformin use associated with lower rates of cardiovascular
    hospitalisation?

## The shared synthetic dataset

All lectures reference the same simulated dataset of ~5,000 people followed
2010-2023. The schema below is exactly what authors should use in code
examples — it keeps the series coherent.

### Table 1: `person` (one row per person)

| Column       | Type    | Notes                                |
|--------------|---------|--------------------------------------|
| person_id    | integer | Primary key.                         |
| birth_date   | date    | ISO-8601.                            |
| sex          | chr     | "M" or "F".                          |
| region       | chr     | "north", "south", "east", "west".    |
| death_date   | date/NA | NA if the person is still alive.     |

### Table 2: `patient_registry` (one row per admission or outpatient visit)

| Column           | Type    | Notes                                                  |
|------------------|---------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| person_id        | integer | Foreign key to person.                                 |
| admission_date   | date    | Start of the encounter.                                |
| discharge_date   | date/NA | NA for an outpatient visit.                            |
| icd10_main       | chr     | Main diagnosis, e.g. "E11.9".                          |
| icd10_secondary  | chr     | ";"-separated secondary diagnoses (may be NA).         |
| ward             | chr     | "medicine", "surgery", "cardio", "emergency", ...      |
| hospital_id      | integer | Hospital.                                              |

### Table 3: `prescription_registry` (one row per dispensing)

| Column           | Type    | Notes                                                  |
|------------------|---------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| person_id        | integer | Foreign key to person.                                 |
| dispensing_date  | date    | When the drug was picked up at the pharmacy.           |
| atc_code         | chr     | ATC classification, e.g. "A10BA02".                    |
| product_name     | chr     | Brand or generic, e.g. "Metformin 500mg".              |
| ddd_dispensed    | numeric | Defined daily doses dispensed for this fill.           |
| prescriber_type  | chr     | "GP", "specialist", "hospital".                        |

Authors can invent plausible loader code like `readr::read_csv("person.csv")`
or `arrow::read_parquet("prescription_registry.parquet")`. The point is
consistency, not realism — the dataset is a shared fiction.

## Style conventions (all lectures)

Every lecture file must start with:

    ::: defaults(skin=playful)

    ::: intro(text="<lecture title>", subtitle="Health + R · Lecture N of 10", enter=blur-in, duration=3500, speak="<lecture title spoken>")

And end with:

    ::: xplainer_link(url="<next lecture file name>", text="Next: <next lecture title>")

(Lecture 10 links back to lecture 1.)

**Hard rules for every lecture:**

- **Tidyverse**: use dplyr, ggplot2, lubridate, tidyr. snake_case variable
  names. Pipe with `|>` (native pipe) or `%>%`, pick one and stick with it.
- **Code blocks**: use `::: r(auto_run=before, title="...")` for
  demonstrations. End-of-line comments in the code become narration.
- **Block variety**: at least **6 distinct block types** per lecture. Do not
  produce a wall of `write_speak`.
- **One comprehension check**: every lecture includes at least one
  `::: multiple_choice` or `::: question`.
- **Playtime target**: 5-8 minutes (~15-25 blocks total).
- **Build on prior lectures**: when you use a concept from a prior lecture,
  mention it by number (e.g. "Remember from Lecture 3, we join by
  `person_id`"). Do not re-derive it from scratch.
- **Tee up the next**: in the closing narration, name what the next lecture
  will add.
- **No fabricated blocks**: only use blocks listed in `src/explain_prompt3.txt`.

## The ten lectures

### 01 — Welcome to health registries (why R?)
**File**: `01_welcome.txt`. **Hook**: what questions can a linked national
health registry answer that a clinical trial can't? Introduce the three-table
schema as a whole. Motivate R as the tool of choice: open, rectangular, strong
tidyverse + survival ecosystem. No code yet beyond one tiny loading snippet.

### 02 — Loading and exploring registry data
**File**: `02_loading.txt`. Read the three tables with `readr` and briefly
mention `data.table` and `arrow` for bigger data. Show `glimpse()`,
`summary()`, `skimr::skim()`. Walk through the shape of each table. Flag the
obvious missing-data patterns (discharge_date NA for outpatient, death_date NA
for alive, icd10_secondary can be NA or `";"`-separated).

### 03 — Linking patients across tables
**File**: `03_linking.txt`. Joins with `dplyr` (`left_join`, `inner_join`,
`anti_join`). Why `left_join` is the default for epidemiology. Date parsing
with `lubridate::ymd()`. Pivoting with `tidyr::pivot_longer`. Show how a
person's story gets reconstructed by joining the three tables on `person_id`.

### 04 — Building a cohort
**File**: `04_cohort.txt`. Inclusion and exclusion criteria: adults with at
least one E11 diagnosis between 2012 and 2020, alive at the time of diagnosis.
Deduplication: pick the **first-ever** E11 record per person (that date is the
**index date**). Introduce the concept of the index date formally — it
recurs in every subsequent lecture.

### 05 — Describing your cohort (Table 1)
**File**: `05_table1.txt`. Counts, proportions, means by group. Use
`gtsummary::tbl_summary()` for a publication-ready Table 1 and show ggplot2
bar/histograms for sex, age-at-index, region. What a reviewer expects in a
Table 1 and what they do not.

### 06 — Measuring drug exposure
**File**: `06_exposure.txt`. From raw dispensing rows to "on drug" time.
Filter prescription_registry to `atc_code == "A10BA02"`. First fill date per
person. Gap-based episode construction: consecutive fills within N days count
as one continuous episode. Introduce DDD as a coarse dose measure.

### 07 — Outcomes in patient registries
**File**: `07_outcomes.txt`. Defining an outcome from ICD-10. Walk through
detecting hospitalisation for major cardiovascular events (I21 acute MI, I63
ischaemic stroke) in `icd10_main` OR `icd10_secondary`. First-occurrence vs
recurrent events. The `";"`-split trick for secondary diagnoses.

### 08 — Follow-up time and censoring
**File**: `08_followup.txt`. Start of follow-up = index date (from Lecture 4).
End = first outcome OR death OR end-of-study (2023-12-31). Introduce
`survival::Surv()` and the concept of censoring. Compute person-time in years.
Common beginner mistakes: including people before their index date, forgetting
to censor at death.

### 09 — Incidence rates and Kaplan-Meier
**File**: `09_rates.txt`. Crude incidence rate per 1000 person-years. Hand-
compute one, then show the `epitools::rateratio()` or base R one-liner.
Kaplan-Meier curves with `survival::survfit()` + `survminer::ggsurvplot()`.
How to read the curve. Median follow-up time vs median survival time.

### 10 — A mini study
**File**: `10_mini_study.txt`. Putting it all together: a reproducible mini-
analysis asking "do metformin users have lower rates of major cardiovascular
events than non-users in our cohort?". End-to-end script: cohort → exposure →
outcome → follow-up → rate ratio with 95% CI. Critical discussion of
confounding by indication, immortal-time bias, and what a proper study would
add. Tee up further reading. Last block links back to Lecture 1.

## Authoring notes

- Every author: read `src/explain_prompt3.txt` first and follow it.
- Every author: keep the running example (type 2 diabetes + metformin)
  visible in at least one block of your lecture — even if your topic is
  "joins", show a join on the metformin prescription table.
- Do not recycle the same opening hook as another lecture.
- Vary the transition styles in `new_page(transition=...)` across lectures
  so the series feels musical, not repetitive.
