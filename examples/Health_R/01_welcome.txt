::: write_speak
Here is a question. Out of five million people, followed for ten years: how often do those who start metformin end up hospitalised with a heart attack, compared to those who don't? No one consented. No one dropped out. Nobody was randomised. Yet the answer is sitting on a hard drive somewhere, waiting.

::: message(level=info, icon=lightbulb)
A randomised trial of metformin with ten-year cardiovascular follow-up on five million people would cost billions and take a decade. A registry study can start tomorrow afternoon.

::: new_page(title="What is a health registry?", transition=wipe-left)

::: write_speak
In Nordic-style healthcare systems, every contact leaves a trace. Every admission, every outpatient visit, every pharmacy pickup is recorded against a unique personal identifier. Researchers with ethics approval can link those traces into one long story per person.

::: write_speak
Three tables do most of the work in this series. One row per person. One row per hospital encounter. One row per prescription pickup. That is the whole universe.

::: new_page(title="The three tables", transition=cross-dissolve)

::: mermaid
erDiagram
    PERSON ||--o{ PATIENT_REGISTRY : "has visits"
    PERSON ||--o{ PRESCRIPTION_REGISTRY : "picks up drugs"
    PERSON {
      int person_id PK
      date birth_date
      chr sex
      chr region
      date death_date
    }
    PATIENT_REGISTRY {
      int person_id FK
      date admission_date
      date discharge_date
      chr icd10_main
      chr icd10_secondary
      chr ward
    }
    PRESCRIPTION_REGISTRY {
      int person_id FK
      date dispensing_date
      chr atc_code
      chr product_name
      num ddd_dispensed
    }

::: write_speak
The magic is `person_id`. It is the same integer in all three tables. Join on it and a patient's timeline snaps into focus — birth, diagnoses, drug pickups, hospital stays, and eventually, sometimes, a death date.

::: new_page(title="Why R?", transition=wipe-right)

::: write_speak
Registry data is rectangular. Rectangular data is what the tidyverse was built for. Add the `survival` package, which is the gold standard for time-to-event analysis, and the whole toolchain is free, scriptable, and reproducible on any laptop.

::: new_page(title="A first glimpse", transition=fade)

**The nine lectures that follow**

1. **Loading and exploring** — `readr`, `glimpse`, and the shape of each table.
2. **Linking patients** — joins across the three tables on `person_id`.
3. **Building a cohort** — defining type 2 diabetes patients and the index date.
4. **Describing your cohort** — a publication-ready Table 1.
5. **Measuring drug exposure** — from raw pickups to metformin episodes.
6. **Defining outcomes** — detecting cardiovascular events from ICD-10 codes.
7. **Follow-up time and censoring** — the `Surv()` object, carefully.
8. **Incidence rates and Kaplan-Meier** — putting numbers on risk.
9. **A mini study** — the whole pipeline, end to end, with caveats.

::: multiple_choice
Which question is best answered with a linked health registry?
* Over 10 years of follow-up, are people who fill metformin less likely to be hospitalised for a heart attack than people who don't?
- In a 12-week trial, does a new drug lower HbA1c more than placebo in 200 volunteers?
- Does a single educational pamphlet improve diet recall the next morning?
